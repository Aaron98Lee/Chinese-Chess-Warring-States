## 1. Method

### 1.1 Evidence hierarchy

Sources are ranked as required by the project brief:

1. Museum collections and object catalogues
2. Archaeological excavation reports
3. Academic publications and university research
4. Historical military studies
5. Reputable reference works
6. Documented surviving artefacts
7. Carefully attributed reconstructions

### 1.2 Classification

**Evidence class** (what kind of support a design element has):

| Class | Meaning | Use in design |
|---|---|---|
| **E1** | Direct evidence: excavated artefact, period depiction, or primary text | May be depicted as historical |
| **E2** | Scholarly reconstruction or interpretation in academic work | May be depicted; Codex says "reconstructed" |
| **E3** | Reasoned design interpretation, extrapolated from E1/E2 close in time and place | Allowed; Codex says "interpretation" |
| **E4** | Artistic invention or game convention, with no historical support | Allowed for non-identity details or with explicit approval; never presented as history |

**Confidence**: High, Medium or Low.

**Verification status in this session:**

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **V2** | Relevant content confirmed online during this research pass |
| **V1** | Existence or bibliographic details confirmed, or content confirmed only by a secondary source |
| **V0** | Not yet verified in this session (domain knowledge). **Must be verified before design lock** |

> **Research limitation:** the environment's network policy blocks the Chinese Text Project (ctext.org) and Wikisource. Classical-text references marked V0 or V1 must be checked against critical editions in Milestone 1. No quotation goes into shipped content until it is V2.

### 1.3 Use of modern screen references (decision D2)

You asked us to refer to ***Qin Dynasty Epic* (大秦賦, 2020)** for weapons, army types and dress. Production coverage reports more than 11,000 costumes and 4,000 sets of armour, many props modelled on archaeological artefacts ([newhanfu.com](https://www.newhanfu.com/9739.html), V1). We will use it as follows:

| Use | Allowed? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Art-direction reference**: overall mood, colour coding of states, how armies read on screen | **Yes** | This is what you asked for, and it helps align the look with what Chinese audiences recognise |
| **Pointer to evidence**: when the drama shows an item, we trace it to the artefact it was based on and cite that artefact | **Yes** | The artefact, not the drama, enters the register |
| **Historical evidence in its own right** | **No** | A modern production is an interpretation. Under the project's own evidence hierarchy it cannot be cited as E1 or E2 |
| **Copying specific costume, armour, prop or emblem designs** | **No** | Production designs are copyrighted creative works. We derive palettes and general looks; our designs are original and evidence-based |

Where the drama departs from the archaeological evidence, the register records the departure and the evidence wins, unless you explicitly approve the screen version as an E4 convention.

**Status:** I could not find published documentation of the drama's state colour coding in this session. The art team will compile a reference sheet from legitimately obtained stills in Milestone 1, for internal use only, and the state palette (§3.3) will be reconciled with it.

## 2. Setting: dating the visual "snapshot"

### 2.1 Approved scope

The game is set in the **Warring States period (475–221 BCE)**. Each player, human or computer, chooses one of the **seven states**. State choice is cosmetic and thematic: **it never changes the Xiangqi rules**.

### 2.2 Recommended visual snapshot: late Warring States, c. 300–230 BCE (decision D15)

The period spans about 250 years, and military equipment changed considerably within it. To avoid mixing equipment from different decades, as the brief requires, all formations should depict **one consistent snapshot**:

| Piece | Dating constraint | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Horse (cavalry)** | True cavalry spreads only after King Wuling of Zhao's "Hu dress and mounted archery" reform, c. 307 BCE (E1 text, V1) | Cavalry formations are anachronistic before about 300 BCE |
| **Chariot** | Still fielded throughout; declining relative to infantry and cavalry | Compatible with any snapshot |
| **Crossbow unit** | Crossbows well established in the 4th–3rd centuries BCE | Compatible |
| **Seven states coexisting** | Han (韓) falls to Qin in 230 BCE, the first of the six | All seven exist only before 230 BCE |
| **Best visual evidence** | Terracotta Army (≤ 210 BCE), late Chu tombs, Yan Xiadu, Fangmatan | Concentrated in the late period |

**Recommendation:** depict the military culture of **c. 300–230 BCE, centred on the era of the Battle of Changping (260 BCE)**. The game's framing still says "Warring States, 475–221 BCE", and the Codex explains that the formations show the late-period armies.

## 3. The seven states

### 3.1 Evidence strength: an honest assessment

The states are **not equally documented**. Qin and Chu have rich figurative and artefact evidence. The others rely more on texts and scattered finds, so their formations will carry more E3 interpretation. This must be visible in the register and the Codex, and it affects art-production risk (R18).

| State | Capital (late period) | Regional script group | Evidence base | Overall strength |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Qin 秦** | Xianyang 咸陽 | Qin | Terracotta Army (thousands of figures, weapons, chariots); stone armour pit K9801; Qin law slips; Fangmatan maps | **Very strong (E1, figurative)** |
| **Chu 楚** | Ying 郢 (to 278 BCE), later Chen and Shouchun | Chu | Zeng Hou Yi tomb (armour, shields, weapons, chariot fittings); Baoshan, Mashan, Jiuliandun tombs; lacquer; *Chu Ci* | **Strong (E1 artefacts)** |
| **Qi 齊** | Linzi 臨淄 | Qi | *Xunzi* on Qi "skilled strikers" (技擊); Sun Bin and the Maling campaign (*Shiji*); Yinqueshan slips of *Sun Bin's Art of War*; *Han Feizi* on Qi purple | Medium (texts + finds) |
| **Zhao 趙** | Handan 邯鄲 | Jin (三晉) | King Wuling's 307 BCE reform (*Shiji*); Li Mu's border armies; Changping battlefield remains | Medium (texts + finds) |
| **Yan 燕** | Ji 薊 / Xiadu 下都 | Yan | Yan Xiadu site (iron weapons and armour; tomb 44 excavation report, 1996); inscribed Yan dagger-axes | Medium (artefacts) |
| **Wei 魏** | Daliang 大梁 | Jin (三晉) | *Xunzi* on the Wei "martial soldiers" (武卒) and their equipment; Wu Qi tradition | Medium–low (texts) |

### 3.2 State identity layer

Each state gets a distinct identity built on a shared Warring States base kit (document 05 §6.5). The **signature trait** is the one thing that should make a state recognisable from the gameplay camera.

| State | Signature trait | Supporting traits | Basis | Class |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Qin** | Terracotta typology: bareheaded infantry with side topknots, small riveted lamellar | Officer caps by rank; crossbows; black-dominant banners | Terracotta Army | E1 |
| **Chu** | Black-lacquered laced rawhide lamellae with vermilion painting; painted dragon-and-phoenix shields | Multi-blade halberds; tall caps for commanders | Zeng Hou Yi; *Xunzi*; *Chu Ci* | E1 / E2 |
| **Qi** | "Skilled strikers": lighter infantry with sword and shield, purple textiles | Fewer heavy armours; agile poses | *Xunzi* (技擊); *Han Feizi* (purple) | E1 text / E3 |
| **Zhao** | "Hu dress": short narrow-sleeved jackets, trousers and boots; mounted archers | Belt hooks; bows prominent in cavalry and chariots | King Wuling's reform | E1 text / E2–E3 |
| **Yan** | Iron: dark iron helmets and weapons | Northern elements (felt, fur trims) | Yan Xiadu finds | E1 / E3 |
| **Wei** | "Martial soldiers": heavily armoured, helmeted infantry carrying heavy crossbows and dagger-axes | Marching packs | *Xunzi* (武卒) | E1 text / E3 |
| **Han** | Heavy crossbows and fine swords | Elite crossbowmen with large prods | *Zhanguo ce* / *Shiji* (strong bows and crossbows from Han) | E1 text / E3 |

**English naming:** the state **Han 韓** shares its romanisation with the later Han dynasty 漢. In English UI and documents we write **"Han (韓)"** and never use the dynasty's character. The UI can use "Hán" with the tone mark if playtests show confusion.

### 3.3 State colours (decision D2): a declared game convention

Battle colours of the Warring States armies are almost entirely **unattested**. The Terracotta figures were painted in varied colours, not uniforms (HR-15). State colours are therefore an **E4 game convention**, anchored to evidence where any exists and otherwise chosen for maximum contrast. They will be reconciled with the *Qin Dynasty Epic* reference in Milestone 1.

| State | Proposed colour | Anchor | Class |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Qin** | Black 玄 | Water-virtue decree favouring black (*Shiji*, 221 BCE; historicity debated); long-standing screen convention | E1 text (late) + E4 |
| **Chu** | Vermilion 朱 | South is associated with red in the five-direction colour scheme; the Chu ancestor Zhurong was a fire deity; red is prominent in Chu lacquer. A "Chu favoured red" hypothesis exists in scholarship | E2 (low) + E4 |
| **Qi** | Purple 紫 | *Han Feizi*: "Duke Huan of Qi liked to wear purple; the whole state wore purple" (anecdote) | E1 text (anecdote) + E4 |
| **Wei** | Ochre-gold 黃 | Wei lay in the central plains; centre is associated with yellow in the five-direction scheme | E4 |
| **Zhao** | Ivory 素 with dark trim | Chosen for contrast | E4 |
| **Yan** | Azure 青 | Chosen for contrast | E4 |
| **Han** | Green 綠 | Chosen for contrast | E4 |

The five-direction colour scheme (east green-blue, south red, west white, north black, centre yellow) is described in the Warring States technical treatise *Kaogong ji* (考工記) (E1 text, V0). It cannot cover seven states cleanly: Qin in the west would be white, and two northern states would compete for black. It is therefore used only as a secondary anchor.

**Colour accessibility:** seven hues cannot all be told apart by colour-blind players (for example Chu vermilion against Han green, or Yan azure against Han green). Ownership therefore never depends on state colour. A **turn-role base band** (first player against second player), the state emblem and the silhouette carry ownership in every pairing (document 05 §4).

### 3.4 State emblems in regional scripts

Warring States writing was regionally differentiated. Scholarship following Wang Guowei distinguishes Qin script from that of the eastern states, now usually divided into five groups: **Qin, Chu, Qi, Yan and Jin** (the Jin group covers Han 韓, Zhao and Wei) (E2, V2). **Each state's emblem is its own name written in its regional script style**: a period-authentic identity mark, distinct from modern UI text. Glyph forms will be designed with a palaeography consultant (Milestone 1).

## 4. Research findings by topic (common Warring States military culture)

### 4.1 Organisation and command

- Armies were organised in nested units, starting from the five-man squad (伍) and ten-man file (什) (E1/E2). Qin merit ranks followed the system introduced under Shang Yang (E1, Shuihudi Qin slips; V0).
- Signals: **drums to advance, metal instruments to withdraw** (*Xunzi*, 議兵; V0).
- **Design:** each Soldier piece is one five-man squad; the general's formation includes a drummer.

### 4.2 Infantry by state

- **Qin:** Terracotta infantry: armoured and unarmoured, mostly **bareheaded** with topknots; robes, trousers, shin guards (E1; HR-09, HR-11).
- **Chu:** lacquered rawhide armour (*Xunzi*: shark skin and rhinoceros hide, V0; *Chu Ci* "Hymn to the Fallen": rhinoceros-hide armour and Wu dagger-axes, V2) (HR-06, HR-07).
- **Wei:** *Xunzi* describes the Wei "martial soldiers" as wearing three layers of armour and a helmet, carrying a twelve-*dan* crossbow, fifty bolts, a dagger-axe, a sword and three days' rations, and marching about 100 *li* in a day (E1 text; V1 via secondary source) (HR-43).
- **Qi:** *Xunzi* says Qi valued "skilled strikers" (技擊), rewarded per enemy head taken (E1 text, V0) (HR-44).
- **Zhao, Yan, Han:** less figurative evidence. Kits are extrapolated from common Warring States finds plus state-specific texts and artefacts (E3).

### 4.3 Cavalry

- Terracotta cavalrymen: short armour vests, caps tied under the chin, saddled horses **without stirrups** (E1; HR-21).
- **Zhao:** King Wuling's reform (c. 307 BCE) adopted the dress of the northern steppe peoples, with short, narrow-sleeved jackets and trousers, for mounted archery (E1 text; V1) (HR-41). Belt hooks are a widely excavated Warring States item (E1), but their link to the reform specifically is not established (E3).
- **Design:** three riders per Horse formation; no stirrups, no horseshoes; Zhao cavalry is the signature cavalry.

### 4.4 Armour and helmets

- **Qin:** small riveted lamellar of role-specific types; stone armour and helmets in pit K9801 (E1; HR-10 to HR-12).
- **Chu:** Zeng Hou Yi lacquered leather armour (13 suits) and reconstruction (E1 + E2; HR-07).
- **Yan:** iron armour and weapons from Yan Xiadu, including tomb 44 (excavation report, Wenwu 1996; V1 bibliographic). Specific helmet details to be verified (HR-42).
- **Design:** armour language is a primary state differentiator: Qin riveted plates, Chu laced lacquer, Yan dark iron, Wei heavy helmeted infantry.

### 4.5 Weapons

- Common to all states (E1): bronze swords (劍), dagger-axes (戈), spears (矛), halberds (戟), sword-spears (鈹), bows, crossbows with bronze triggers. Iron weapons spread during the 3rd century BCE, especially in Yan and Chu (E1/E2).
- Living weapons were **bright bronze or dark iron**, not green patina. Surface chromium on Terracotta bronzes is lacquer contamination, not an ancient anti-rust treatment (E2, V2; HR-13).
- **Han:** famed for strong bows and crossbows and fine swords (*Zhanguo ce*, Han strategies; *Shiji*, biography of Su Qin; V1 via secondary paraphrase) (HR-45).

### 4.6 Crossbow unit (the Cannon piece, decision D4)

This is the strongest-evidenced piece in the game.

| Evidence | Class | Verif. |
|---|---|---|
| Bronze crossbow trigger mechanisms excavated in large numbers from Warring States and Qin sites | E1 | V0 |
| Terracotta pit 2 crossbow array: kneeling and standing crossbowmen | E1 | V0 |
| Wei martial soldiers carrying twelve-*dan* crossbows (*Xunzi*) | E1 text | V1 |
| Han's strong bows and crossbows (*Zhanguo ce* / *Shiji*) | E1 text | V1 |
| Maling (342 BCE): Sun Bin's ambush with massed crossbowmen (*Shiji*) | E1 text | V0 |

**Gameplay fit:** the Cannon captures by jumping exactly one piece. This is shown as a **lofted crossbow volley over the screen piece**. Lofting a volley is plausible for massed fire, but the visual arc is an artistic choice (E3).

**Overlap rule:** to keep the crossbow piece unique, **Soldier and Advisor formations carry no crossbows**.

### 4.7 Strategist (the Elephant/Minister piece, decision D3)

| Evidence | Class | Verif. |
|---|---|---|
| Sun Bin served Qi's general Tian Ji as military adviser (軍師). The *Shiji* describes him planning from inside a covered baggage cart (辎車); the *Zizhi Tongjian* records "Tian Ji was general and Sun Bin was adviser" (孫子為師) | E1 text | V1 (*Zizhi Tongjian* via secondary); V0 (cart passage) |
| The great retainer households of the period (e.g. the Lords of Mengchang, Pingyuan, Xinling and Chunshen) kept large numbers of advisers and retainers (*Shiji*) | E1 text | V0 |
| **Maps on wooden boards** from Fangmatan tomb 1, a late Warring States Qin tomb at Tianshui, excavated 1986 | E1 | V2 |
| Writing on bamboo and wooden slips (Shuihudi, Baoshan) | E1 | V0 |

**Naming refinement (for confirmation, D3):** you referred to the role as **參軍 / 謀士**. **參軍 ("military counsellor") is a later title.** It is attested from the end of the Eastern Han (for example the *Sanguozhi* records Yuan Shao appointing someone 參軍事), about four centuries after the Warring States (V1). To avoid an anachronism in in-world text, we propose **軍師 (military adviser) / 謀士 (strategist)**. They express exactly the role you described, and the game's role description can still explain it as a staff adviser.

**Design:** a strategist in civilian robe and cap, kneeling on a mat or standing, holding a wooden map board; an aide with bamboo-slip bundles; an attendant holding a round parasol canopy. Canopies are attested on chariots (Qin bronze chariots, E1); a hand-held canopy is an interpretation (E3), chosen because a round disc reads clearly from above. **Gameplay link:** like the strategist who plans from behind the lines, this piece cannot cross the river.

### 4.8 Chariots

- Warring States chariots share a common design: two large spoked wheels, a single draught pole, a rectangular box and four-horse teams for battle chariots (E1/E2). The Qin bronze chariots and pit chariots give the best detail (E1; HR-16); Chu chariot finds supply the Chu variant (HR-17).
- Chariot burials are also known in other states' territories (e.g. Qi at Linzi; Wei-area finds), with details to be verified (V0).
- **Design:** one shared chariot base with state-specific fittings, decoration, crew kit and banners.

### 4.9 Banners, insignia and colour

- Battlefield textiles do not survive. Banner forms come from period depictions: inlaid bronze vessels with battle scenes show pennants with feather or tassel finials (E1 depiction, V0; HR-32).
- State colours are a game convention (§3.3). State emblems use regional scripts (§3.4).

### 4.10 Music and signals

Drums advance; metal instruments withdraw (HR-33). The Zeng Hou Yi chime bells provide period timbre and tuning (E1, V1; HR-34). Erhu-type instruments are later and excluded.

### 4.11 Battlefield environment (decision D18)

- **Recommended MVP battlefield: the Dan River valley at Changping (長平, near modern Gaoping, Shanxi), 260 BCE.** It was the largest battle of the period, it has a real river to form the board's river, and the Zhao defensive lines and Qin camps are traditionally identified there (E1 site; V0). Mass-grave excavations near Gaoping, such as the Yonglu pit, are documented (V0). **No human remains are depicted**; the site informs only terrain and fortifications.
- The battlefield is neutral to the chosen states: any pairing can fight there. Additional battlefields (for example Maling, Yique, Jimo) are post-MVP candidates.
- **The river inscription.** The traditional board text 楚河漢界 ("Chu River, Han Border") refers to the Chu–Han war of 206–202 BCE, after the Warring States. **Recommendation:** keep it on the riverbank markers as the board's traditional convention, which every Xiangqi player expects. The Codex explains its origin, and an option replaces it with the battlefield's river name (丹水).

### 4.12 The game itself

Xiangqi was not played in the Warring States. Its earliest literary descriptions are commonly traced to the Tang, and the modern form with the Cannon to the Song (V1; HR-38). Codex text says so plainly.

## 6. Anachronism exclusion list

| Excluded element | Reason |
|---|---|
| Stirrups; horseshoes | Not in use |
| Gunpowder weapons; gun-barrel "cannons" | About a millennium later |
| Trebuchets (dropped under D4) | Not needed; weakly evidenced for field use |
| War elephants (dropped under D3) | No evidence of military use in this period's northern states |
| Pavise shields and other later standing shields | Later periods |
| The title 參軍 in in-world text | Late Eastern Han onward |
| Ring-pommel sabre (環首刀) as the standard sidearm | Becomes common later in the Han |
| Tang, Song, Ming or Qing armour and helmets; plumed later helmets | Later periods |
| *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* iconography (guandao, flowing-beard generals) | Later literary tradition |
| Japanese armour or helmet influences | Wrong culture |
| Imperial yellow dragon banners; Qing-style pennants | Later periods |
| Paper documents | Slips, wooden boards and silk are the period media |
| Chairs and folding stools | People knelt on mats; the folding stool arrived later |
| Green-patina weapons; unpainted grey "terracotta" troops | Museum condition, not period appearance |
| Modern Chinese characters on in-world objects | Regional scripts in-world; modern scripts only in UI |
| Copied costume, prop or emblem designs from screen productions | Copyright and evidence policy (§1.3) |
